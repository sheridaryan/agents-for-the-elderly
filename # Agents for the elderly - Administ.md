2. Your instinct is correct: the adoption problem
You are right that a purely psychological agent faces a serious voluntary adoption problem, and it is worth naming this precisely. Here are two distinct frameworks for understanding it:
Framework 1 — the identity resistance model. Caregivers largely do not self-identify as people in distress. They identify as someone taking care of someone else. An agent positioned as "support for you" triggers the same resistance as therapy-seeking: it requires them to acknowledge a problem with themselves. An agent positioned as "help managing Mum's care" does not. The practical entry point bypasses identity resistance.
Framework 2 — the cognitive load model. Caregivers, particularly in acute phases, have severely depleted executive function and bandwidth. Asking them to engage with self-care content when they are in the middle of managing a medication error, a hospital discharge, or a wandering episode is cognitively inaccessible. But asking them to do a practical task (track a medication, prepare for an appointment, update the family) meets them where their attention actually is. Once that practical task is completed, there is a natural opening — the stress that was driving the behaviour — where psychological support can be offered without friction.
The research supports this. Qualitative work on digital health technology adoption by caregivers found that challenges were compounded by inadequate design that failed to meet caregivers at their actual point of need, Frontiers and that cultural and contextual factors strongly moderated what felt accessible. The practical task is the door. The psychological support is what is on the other side.

3. Three scenarios where practical and psychological combine at highest value
Rather than cataloguing all possibilities, I want to give you three scenarios that represent the highest-value integration points — where the practical and psychological are so tightly bound that separating them loses something essential from both.
________________________________________
Scenario A: The medication complexity moment
The situation. The caregiver has a parent with heart failure and type 2 diabetes — your priority chronic disease cluster. There are now eleven medications, two of which have just been changed following a cardiology appointment. The caregiver is sitting in the pharmacy parking lot, confused about whether the new beta-blocker replaces or supplements the old one, worried about a drug interaction they half-remember reading about, and furious that the cardiologist's office gave contradictory instructions to what the GP said.
What the practical layer does. The agent holds a current, structured medication record. It can surface the two conflicting instructions, flag the potential interaction for the caregiver to raise with the pharmacist, and generate a question list for the prescriber. It can send an updated medication summary to any family member who helps with care. This alone reduces the immediate cognitive load substantially.
Where the psychological layer enters naturally. After the practical task resolves — or mid-way through, when the caregiver says something like "I can't keep doing this, I'm the only one managing all of this" — the agent has both the context and the relational opening to validate. It already knows from the medication record that there are eleven drugs across two specialists and a GP. It doesn't need to ask "what's wrong?" It can say: "You're managing an extremely complex regimen across conflicting advice from two specialists. That's not a personal failing — it's a system failure landing on you." This is D1 (validation) emerging organically from a practical transaction, not as a scheduled psychological session.
The guilt mechanism. This scenario is also a classic decision-guilt trigger (D6). The caregiver may wonder whether they should have caught the contradiction earlier, whether they are "doing it right," whether something will go wrong because of the confusion. The agent, knowing the history of the medication record and the date of the appointment, can contextualise this: "You're catching this within 24 hours of the appointment. That's exactly what monitoring looks like." This is cognitive reappraisal embedded in a practical event, not offered abstractly.

Scenario B: The appointment preparation and debrief cycle
The situation. A distance caregiver — living two hours away — has arranged to travel to attend her father's neurology follow-up for his Parkinson's disease. She has not been to an appointment in four months. She has a list of concerns in her head but has never written them down. She is anxious about what the neurologist will say, worried she won't remember everything, and already anticipating a difficult conversation with her brother who disagrees with her about whether their father needs more support.
What the practical layer does. In the days before the appointment, the agent runs a structured pre-appointment preparation: "What has changed since the last visit? What have you noticed?" It structures the caregiver's observations into a clinical summary the neurologist can actually use. It generates a prioritised question list. It holds a record of what was discussed last time and what was supposed to happen (referrals, test results, medication adjustments). On the day, it can send a reminder with the key questions. After the appointment, it captures what was decided, what needs follow-up, and who is responsible for what.
Where the psychological layer enters naturally. The pre-appointment conversation surfaces something important: the caregiver has not spoken to her brother in three weeks, and she's dreading the post-appointment family call. This is not a separate psychological problem — it is directly embedded in the appointment logistics. The agent can acknowledge this explicitly: "Managing a care decision with a sibling who sees things differently is one of the hardest parts of distance caregiving." This is D4 (social isolation — family fracture rather than social disconnection from friends) entered through a logistics conversation. The brother is not the problem; the isolation and the absence of shared understanding is.
The debrief. After the appointment, the caregiver is likely emotionally activated — particularly if the news was difficult (progression, new symptoms, a recommendation they weren't expecting). The agent already knows what was discussed because it captured it. It can open the post-appointment conversation by acknowledging the emotional weight before returning to the practical follow-up: "Before we get to next steps — how was that for you?" This sequencing (validate first, task second) honours the core therapeutic principle without requiring a separate session.
Why this is high-value for distance caregivers specifically. The preparation-debrief cycle solves a problem that is unique and severe for distance caregivers: they often attend appointments with insufficient context, leave without capturing decisions clearly, and then have to reconstruct and relay the information to other family members under emotional duress. The agent structures this entire cycle. And because distance caregivers carry disproportionate guilt about not being present day-to-day, the structured competence the agent provides is itself a psychological intervention — it reduces the guilt-inducing perception of inadequacy.

Scenario C: The behavioural incident crisis-debrief loop (dementia-specific)
The situation. A cohabiting caregiver of a person with moderate dementia has just managed a three-hour episode of agitation, repeated accusations, and wandering attempts. It is 11pm. The elder is finally asleep. The caregiver is exhausted, shaken, and has no one to tell.
Why this is the highest-stakes integration point. This is the scenario where both practical deficits and emotional collapse occur simultaneously and at their most intense. The caregiver needs two things in this moment: to make sense of what just happened practically (was this a new symptom? should the GP be called?), and to have a witness to their emotional experience (they are likely experiencing acute distress, possibly with elements of D2 grief — the person they knew is gone — and D6 self-blame — did they handle it right?).
What the practical layer does. The agent runs a structured post-incident capture: time, trigger, duration, behaviours, what helped, what didn't. This serves three purposes. It creates a clinical record the GP and neurologist need (behavioural escalation is often under-documented because it happens at night and feels chaotic). It gives the caregiver a structured task to do with their distress — channelling it rather than leaving it formless. And it generates a risk assessment for whether the incident warrants a clinical contact in the next 24 hours.
Where the psychological layer enters. The act of capturing the incident together is itself therapeutic. The caregiver is not alone — there is a witness. The agent can reflect back what was captured: "You managed a three-hour episode, found what worked, kept him safe, and now you're still processing it at 11pm. That's the reality of this kind of care." This is D1 (being witnessed) at precisely the moment when it is most needed and when a human witness is least available.
The guilt mechanism again. Post-incident guilt is clinically well-documented in dementia caregiving. The caregiver will almost certainly replay the episode wondering what they could have done differently. The agent, having captured the incident data, can gently and specifically address this: "You tried redirection twice before the distraction worked — that's exactly the response sequence the research suggests." This is D6 cognitive reappraisal emerging from a practical record, not from a separate therapeutic session.
The clinical handoff. If the incident log shows escalating frequency (which the agent can track across incidents), the burnout detection layer (D5) has real data to work with. The transition from "this agent witnessed my hard night" to "I think we should talk to your GP about how frequently this is happening" is a natural, relational escalation — not a cold clinical referral.

What this tells us about architecture
The three scenarios reveal a consistent pattern that should reshape your architectural thinking. The practical task is not a separate module that runs alongside the psychological support — it is the entry point from which psychological support becomes accessible. The structure is:
Practical task → relational presence → psychological opening → resolution or handoff
This is not unique to this agent. It is how good social workers, care coordinators, and community nurses actually work. They never lead with "how are you feeling?" They lead with "let's sort out the medication" — and the emotional work happens in the space created by that practical engagement.
What this means for your design is that the practical features are not simply features to increase adoption. They are psychological delivery mechanisms. The medication record is also the guilt-reappraisal substrate. The appointment preparation is also the anxiety-management protocol. The incident log is also the witnessing relationship. If you build the practical layer without the psychological layer, you have Caring Village. If you build the psychological layer without the practical layer, you have an adoption problem. If you build both as a unified loop — practical task surfaces emotional content, emotional content is met before returning to practical resolution — you have something that does not exist yet.
The question for the next design step is: which two or three practical features carry the most psychological freight? I would nominate medication management, appointment preparation/debrief, and incident/behaviour logging as the three highest-value integration points, and suggest we design those as the core of the combined agent before expanding. Practical Domain A: Communication — Family, Friends, and Healthcare Professionals
The problem this domain is actually solving
Communication is the most pervasive invisible labour in caregiving, and the most under-designed-for. Qualitative research consistently finds that caregivers of older adults with multiple chronic conditions support health management in several key ways, including monitoring conditions, communicating with clinicians, and tracking health information — and that conflicted relationships within families resulted in less involved and less effective caregivers. PubMed Central The communication burden is threefold and each strand has a different character.
With healthcare professionals, the fundamental problem is asymmetry of access and credibility. Research on family participation in primary care identifies major challenges including potential for diminished patient autonomy, within-family disagreements, and complex clinician-patient-caregiver dynamics — with studies finding that patients hold negative views toward approaches that exclude them even when they are present. PubMed Central The caregiver is often the most informed person in the room about the elder's day-to-day condition, yet is structurally positioned as secondary. Information they have gathered over weeks is relayed verbally in a ten-minute appointment window. Conversely, they leave appointments with instructions they partially understood, sometimes contradicting what another specialist said, with no record of what was actually agreed.
With family members, the problem is distributed burden, unequal involvement, and conflict. Research on dementia caregiving families identifies that many family conflicts among siblings involved accusations of neglect and financial mismanagement, and that family conflict prior to diagnosis predicted worse outcomes during caregiving. PubMed Central Caregivers who used indirect support-seeking strategies — not directly asking for help — reported greater burden, with unsupportive responses from network members partially mediating this relationship. Sage Journals The structural problem is that the primary caregiver often cannot directly ask for help, does not know how to distribute it, and is blamed for decisions made under conditions others don't observe.
With friends and broader social networks, the problem is communicative collapse. Once caregiving absorbs identity and time, social communication shrinks — not because the caregiver doesn't want it, but because the content of their daily life feels unshareable, or too heavy for social contexts. This connects directly to D4 (social isolation), but the mechanism is communicative, not just relational.

Three features of the Communication domain
Feature A1: The Structured Update — shared situational awareness across the care network
The most practically valuable communication feature in the market, as demonstrated by both Caring Village (wellness journal, village broadcast) and ianacare (task coordination), is reducing the burden of repeatedly re-explaining the current situation to different people. The caregiver currently must individually update multiple people — siblings, the elder's GP, a district nurse, their own employer — who all have different levels of context.
The agent's role here is to help the caregiver compose a structured, layered situation update that can be selectively shared. This is not a broadcast system (that raises privacy concerns and creates noise). It is a tiered narrative — a current summary of the elder's situation, the caregiver's current challenges, and specific requests for help — with the caregiver controlling who sees what layer.
The agent produces this through conversation, not form-filling. It might ask: "How is your mum right now compared to last month? What's the most important thing you want people to understand about what's changed?" The output is a readable, human-feeling update the caregiver approves before anything is shared. This reduces the cognitive load of explaining, and removes the guilt about "burdening" others — because the update is there if family members want to read it, and specific asks are explicit rather than implied.
The psychological layer activates when the caregiver struggles to compose the update — which is itself often a signal. If they cannot say "what's the most important thing," it may be because so much is important. If they say "I don't want to worry them," this opens D1 (being witnessed) and D3 (identity) material. The agent can note gently: "You're managing a lot that other people can't see. Would it help to let them in a little, or does that feel like too much right now?"
Feature A2: The Appointment Advocacy Brief — communicating with healthcare professionals
The communication breakdown between informal caregivers and clinicians is well-documented and under-served. Research on clinician-caregiver interaction highlights that patients and caregivers prefer medical visits that involve collaborative rather than individual discussions, and that cognitive impairment is a salient factor reshaping the caregiver's role from companion to active health manager. PubMed Central
The agent prepares a one-page clinical brief before each specialist appointment — structured, objective, time-stamped — that the caregiver can hand to or read from during the appointment. The brief contains four sections: current status and recent changes; behavioural or symptomatic observations since the last appointment; the caregiver's specific concerns or questions; and a medication list with any recent changes or confusions flagged.
This is different from a generic question list. It is drawn from the agent's ongoing record — the incident log, the medication record, the health observations the caregiver has mentioned in check-ins — and translated into clinical language without losing the caregiver's voice. The caregiver reviews and edits it before the appointment. It serves two functions: it maximises the information density of the appointment, and it positions the caregiver as a credible, organised informant rather than an anxious family member.
After the appointment, the agent runs a structured debrief: what was decided, what follow-up is needed, and whether there was anything that felt unclear or upsetting. The clinical follow-up (what the GP said about the new dose, whether the referral was made) is separated from the emotional debrief (how it felt to hear the diagnosis progressing). The agent does the practical follow-up first, then asks: "How are you doing after all of that?"
The post-appointment space is one of the most emotionally loaded moments in caregiving. News — especially about dementia progression, end-stage renal failure, cardiac deterioration — often arrives here. The agent needs to be able to hold both the practical (the next appointment is in 6 weeks, here are the things to watch for) and the emotional (this is a lot to take in, and you're the one who has to drive home with it and then go back to caring).
Feature A3: Negotiating Help — from family and friends
Research on support-seeking tactics in dementia caregiving found that caregivers who used indirect means to gain support experienced higher caregiving burden, partly because indirect approaches reduce the caregiver's sense of control over outcomes. Sage Journals The implication is that the agent needs to help caregivers move from indirect to direct help-seeking — but in a way that does not feel blunt, confrontational, or culturally discordant.
The agent's role is to help the caregiver convert ambiguous resentment into specific, actionable requests. The conversation usually starts with something like "my brother never helps" or "my friends have stopped asking how I'm doing." The agent's function is not to validate the grievance and leave it there (though validation comes first), nor to immediately problem-solve. It is to ask: "If your brother could do one concrete thing in the next two weeks that would actually make a difference, what would it be?" This is motivational interviewing applied to the caregiving network — moving from diffuse frustration toward a specific, achievable ask.
The output, if the caregiver wants it, is a drafted message — an email, a text, a WhatsApp — that makes the specific request in the caregiver's voice, framed in a way that makes it easy for the recipient to say yes. The agent is a communication ghostwriter, not a scheduler. It is not assigning tasks to the brother. It is helping the caregiver say what they need.
The psychological underpinning here draws on D4 (social isolation) and D6 (self-compassion). Many caregivers cannot ask for help because they believe they should not need to, or because asking feels like admitting failure. The agent reframes asking as competence, not weakness: "Knowing what you need and being able to say it clearly is one of the hardest skills in caregiving. Most people never get there."

Practical Domain B: Appointment Preparation, Management, and Debrief
The problem this domain is actually solving
The appointment management problem is one of the most objectively measurable forms of caregiver burden because it has direct, countable costs. For an elder with dementia, heart failure, and type 2 diabetes — your priority cluster — the appointment load can easily run to 15–20 specialist and primary care contacts per year, across at least three separate clinical systems that do not communicate with each other. The informal caregiver is the only person who carries information across all of them.
Qualitative research identifies that organisational support — managing frequent appointments and complex recommendations — is particularly important for caregivers of older adults with multiple chronic conditions, and that this aspect of caregiving has been underexamined compared to ADL and emotional burden. PubMed Central The evidence from the Demensia KITA NGT sessions is also direct: "Appointment and reminders" was the fourth-ranked domain that caregivers themselves nominated, suggesting that appointment management was the most practically urgent of the administrative concerns they raised.
There are three distinct phases of every appointment, and each has a different character, different problems, and different emotional loading.
________________________________________
Feature B1: Pre-appointment preparation
The functional gap. The caregiver arrives at a specialist appointment, often after an hour of travel and managing the elder's anxiety, having carried a list of concerns in their head since the last appointment four months ago. Some of those concerns were urgent six weeks ago and are now resolved; others have emerged in the last week. The clinician has a ten-minute slot. The caregiver forgets half of what they meant to say, feels steamrolled by the clinical agenda, and leaves with a vague sense of incompletion.
What the agent does. In the 48 hours before a scheduled appointment, the agent initiates a preparation conversation calibrated to the appointment type. The conversation is conversational, not form-like. For a cardiology follow-up it might open: "Dr. Okonkwo is next week. Before we put together your questions — what has your dad been like since you last saw him?" This allows the caregiver to surface concerns in their own language, which the agent then structures into a priority-ordered question list.
The agent draws from its ongoing record to surface things the caregiver may have forgotten: the incident log entries from the last three months, medication changes, observations the caregiver mentioned in check-ins that were not formally recorded as medical concerns. It prompts: "You mentioned he was more confused in the mornings a few weeks back — is that still happening? That might be worth raising with the neurologist."
The output is a structured, printable brief (Feature A2 above): a concise summary of changes since the last appointment, a prioritised question list (maximum five questions, ranked by the caregiver), and the current medication list. The agent also prepares the caregiver for what the appointment will likely cover — for a memory clinic follow-up, it might explain what a MMSE re-assessment typically involves and why scores fluctuate, reducing the shock if the score has dropped.
For cohabiting caregivers, the preparation conversation can also address logistics: who is coming, does the elder know what the appointment is for, and how to frame it to minimise anxiety in the journey there. For distance caregivers attending an appointment they rarely join, the agent adds context: what has happened since the last one they attended, what the cohabiting carer (if there is one) has observed, so they do not arrive with a three-month information gap.
The psychological layer. Pre-appointment anxiety is clinically significant and almost universal in caregivers of people with progressive conditions. The agent acknowledges this without pathologising it: "It's completely normal to feel anxious before these appointments — you're about to get information about someone you love." If the caregiver signals high anxiety (language, timing of the message, escalating worry), the agent does not skip over it to complete the checklist. It asks: "Is there something specific you're worried they might say?"

Feature B2: Appointment management and real-time support
The functional gap. The appointment itself is chaotic. The elder may not want to be there. The clinician may speak to the elder as if the caregiver is not present, or speak to the caregiver as if the elder cannot hear — both of which happen, and both of which are distressing. Key information is conveyed quickly. Medical language is used without explanation. Decisions are made under time pressure that the caregiver has not had time to think through.
What the agent does. This is the domain where the agent is least present (the caregiver is in a clinical encounter), but two features are achievable. The first is a voice-memo or post-appointment capture prompt — immediately after leaving the appointment, the agent prompts a voice note: "While it's fresh — what did they say?" This captures the raw content before the caregiver gets home, processes it with other family members, and begins to lose the precise wording. The agent transcribes, structures, and stores this as the appointment record.
The second is preparation for the interaction itself. The agent can coach the caregiver in advance on how to assert presence: how to introduce themselves as the primary caregiver at the start of the appointment, how to interrupt politely if the conversation moves too fast, how to ask for a repetition or clarification without feeling foolish. For caregivers of people with dementia, it can provide specific guidance: that it is appropriate to speak for the elder when the elder cannot give a clear account of symptoms, and that good clinicians will expect this.
The psychological layer. The appointment interaction is often a dignity battlefield, and caregivers frequently feel invisible, dismissed, or inadequately informed. Research finds that patients hold negative views when clinicians direct conversation away from them, and that cognitive impairment reshapes the caregiver role from passive companion to active health manager — a transition that clinicians may not always acknowledge. PubMed Central When the debrief reveals this kind of experience — "she just talked to me the whole time, he was right there" or "he basically told me nothing I could actually use" — the agent validates the experience before moving to problem-solving. This is D1 material: the caregiver was the unacknowledged expert in the room. Naming that is not venting; it is accurate.

Feature B3: Post-appointment debrief and follow-up
The functional gap. After the appointment, the caregiver is holding multiple things simultaneously: the clinical information they received, the emotional weight of the news (particularly if the condition has progressed), the logistical follow-up they need to manage (a referral, a blood test, a prescription change), and the task of relaying all of this to other family members in a way they will understand. They do this alone, usually while also managing the elder's post-appointment agitation or fatigue.
What the agent does. The post-appointment debrief is a two-part conversation: practical, then emotional — in that order, because the practical gives the caregiver something concrete to do with their distress, and finishing it creates space for the emotional. The practical part captures what was decided (new medication, referral made, next appointment date), what needs to happen next (who books the blood test, does the specialist need to send a letter to the GP), and anything that was unclear or contradictory.
The agent then generates two outputs. The first is an appointment summary the caregiver can approve and share with family members — removing the burden of having to re-explain. The second is a follow-up checklist with assigned timelines: "Blood test to be booked by next Friday. New prescription to be collected Monday." These are entered into the agent's ongoing record and will reappear as reminders.
Only once the practical debrief is complete — or if the caregiver signals they need to talk first — does the agent shift register: "Before we close this — how are you doing after all of that?" This is the opening for D2 (news about progression triggering grief), D5 (burnout signal from the weight of managing this), or D6 (guilt if the caregiver feels they should have advocated better). The agent does not always need to go there. Sometimes the caregiver just needs the practical handling done and to get on with their day. The option is always offered; it is never forced.

Practical Domain C: Incident and Behaviour Logging
The problem this domain is actually solving
Incident and behaviour logging is the most distinctively geriatric of the three domains — it is the domain most specific to your priority conditions and most absent from competitors. For dementia and frailty, the clinical management of behavioural and psychological symptoms (BPSD) and functional decline depends entirely on patterns of observation over time. A single incident is ambiguous; a pattern is diagnostic. The problem is that those patterns are currently stored nowhere except in the caregiver's head, or in scattered notes, or not at all.
Research on validation communication in dementia care establishes that challenging behaviours are best understood as expressions of unmet need — including needs for comfort, safety, love, self-esteem, control, and occupation — and that communication is the primary tool for meeting these needs and reducing resistive care behaviours. PubMed Central The clinical implication is that an incident log is not just an administrative record; it is the evidence base for understanding the elder's needs and adjusting the caregiving response.
For physical frailty, the relevant incidents are falls, near-misses, episodes of confusion or delirium, changes in mobility, changes in appetite, and moments of distress during personal care. For chronic disease, they are medication adherence observations, blood pressure and blood glucose readings, breathlessness episodes, and changes in usual function. Each of these has immediate clinical relevance and almost no systematic capture pathway in current caregiver tools.
________________________________________
Feature C1: Conversational incident capture
The functional gap. Existing tools (Caring Village's wellness journal, for example) require the caregiver to open an app, navigate to a logging function, and type a record — usually after an incident that has left them exhausted and emotionally activated. Compliance is predictably low. Clinical logging systems (like those in formal care environments) are even more demanding. The result is that the incident record that reaches the clinician at the next appointment is a verbal summary of the caregiver's imperfect memory, biased toward recent events and emotionally salient ones.
What the agent does. The agent captures incidents through conversation, not a form. It learns from the caregiver's natural language what constitutes an incident worth capturing. After an established relationship, the agent recognises incident-relevant language without requiring the caregiver to categorise it: "He wouldn't let me help him shower this morning, it turned into a whole thing" triggers a gentle prompt: "That sounds difficult. Do you want to take a quick note of what happened — it might be useful to track whether this is becoming more frequent?" The caregiver narrates verbally or by text; the agent structures and stores the record.
The structured record contains: date and time, type of incident (behavioural, falls, medication, personal care resistance, delirium-like symptoms, emotional distress), precipitating context, duration, what was tried, what helped or didn't, and severity (mild/moderate/significant — caregiver-rated). The caregiver does not fill in a form with these fields. The agent asks conversationally and populates the record from the answers.
Over time, the agent builds a pattern analysis capability. It can notice: three episodes of care resistance in the last two weeks, all occurring in the morning; a cluster of agitation episodes in the evenings across the past month; a fall three weeks ago that was not followed up. These patterns, surfaced at the next appointment preparation, become the evidence the clinician needs to adjust management. This closes the loop between the caregiver's daily observation and the clinical encounter — the most significant information gap in current geriatric care.
Evidence base. The STAV mobile app intervention, cited in the project documents, found that caregivers valued the digital diary feature specifically because it "supported reflection and continuity" — professionals found that engagement with structured logging improved their ability to tailor support. The Demensia KITA expert panel similarly identified behavioural problem management as a priority sub-module, with the note that caregivers needed frameworks for understanding and recording behaviour before they could manage it.

Feature C2: Pattern recognition and clinical translation
The functional gap. Even when caregivers do keep informal logs, they cannot interpret what they see. Is three agitation episodes in two weeks a pattern of escalation, or normal variation? Is the increase in nocturnal wandering a new symptom or a response to a recent medication change? Is the weight loss observable to the caregiver clinically significant? Without a framework for interpretation, the log is just noise.
What the agent does. The agent does not diagnose. It provides psychoeducation grounded in the elder's specific conditions. For a caregiver of someone with moderate dementia, it can explain: "Resistance to personal care is one of the most common expressions of unmet need in dementia. It often signals that something about the environment or the approach is activating distress, not that your dad is deliberately difficult. Here are three things people often find helpful." This is the knowledge layer integrated with the log — not generic information about dementia, but information relevant to what has just been recorded.
For the clinician handoff, the agent translates the log into a clinical summary. Not a raw data dump — a prioritised narrative that highlights what has changed, what is new, what is escalating, and what has resolved. The caregiver reviews and approves this. For conditions with clear warning signs — breathlessness worsening in a patient with heart failure, recurrent falls in a frail elder, signs of delirium — the agent can flag explicitly: "The pattern of increased confusion over the past five days is something Dr. Williams should hear about before your next scheduled appointment. Would you like help contacting the surgery today?"

Feature C3: The incident debrief — where logging becomes psychological
This is the most important integration point in the entire domain.
An incident log sounds clinical. But every entry represents a moment of stress, effort, distress, or fear. The caregiver who records "refused shower, became aggressive, shouting, lasted 40 minutes" has just spent 40 minutes managing a distressing situation. They are recording it either immediately after (while still activated) or later that day (possibly after also managing dinner, medications, and bed routine). The act of logging is psychologically loaded.
The agent should never treat incident logging as purely administrative. Every significant incident capture closes with an acknowledgement: "That sounds exhausting. How are you doing now?" This is not therapy. It is the minimum relational dignity the interaction requires.
For patterns that indicate escalating burden — frequency increasing, severity increasing, caregiver-reported distress escalating — the agent's passive detection layer (D5) has real clinical data to work with. The transition from "I need to tell you something about the pattern I'm seeing" to "I think you might be approaching a point where you need some extra support" is grounded in observable data, not a generalised burnout questionnaire. The caregiver cannot dismiss it as generic. The agent can say: "In the last three weeks you've managed six significant incidents. That's a lot. Most people would be struggling at this point. I want to make sure you're getting some support — not just for your dad, but for you."
This is also the primary guilt-activation domain for dementia caregivers. Post-incident guilt — "did I handle that right?", "was I too harsh?", "should I have tried something different?" — is clinically ubiquitous. The incident log, if reviewed with the agent, becomes the evidence for cognitive reappraisal (D6). The agent can look at the record and say, specifically: "You tried redirection, then distraction, then called a break. That's a competent response sequence. The incident resolved. You did not fail." This is not generic reassurance. It is evidence-based reappraisal from the caregiver's own data.

Structural integration across the three domains
The three domains share a single underlying logic that should govern how they are built:
Practical capture creates the psychological substrate. The incident log, the appointment summary, the communication update — each of these generates evidence that the agent can use in psychological conversations. The caregiver who says "I don't know if I'm handling this right" can be met, not with a generic reassurance, but with: "Let me look at what we've captured over the past month." This transforms psychological support from an opinion the agent offers into a reflection the caregiver's own data supports.
The emotional entry point is always the practical task. Caregivers come to these features because they need to prepare for an appointment, log a difficult night, or ask for help from a sibling. They do not come because they want emotional support. The psychological layer must be accessible from within the practical task, not positioned as a separate offering.
The three domains are clinically interdependent. The incident log feeds the appointment brief. The appointment debrief updates the incident record. The communication update to family members draws from both. These are not three features — they are three interfaces on a single caregiving situation model. The agent holds the whole picture; each feature surfaces a different part of it.

A few things worth pausing on before moving forward.
On the clinical brief (Feature A2/B1): One design tension specific to the North American context is HIPAA and its Canadian equivalents. The agent can help the caregiver compose an advocacy brief and prepare questions — but the question of who shares what with which clinician, and whether the caregiver has formal substitute decision-maker status, matters clinically and legally. The agent should be explicit at onboarding that it is helping the caregiver communicate, not acting as a clinical intermediary. The brief is a document the caregiver chooses to share; the agent does not transmit it to anyone.
On incident logging and safeguarding: The evidence you cited on family conflict and elder abuse deserves a flag here. The incident log could theoretically capture patterns that raise safeguarding concerns — not necessarily abuse, but caregiver overwhelm reaching a point where safety is compromised. The agent's D5 escalation protocol already handles the caregiver's own crisis. The situation model needs a parallel, carefully designed safeguarding flag — one that treats the caregiver with dignity (they are overwhelmingly not perpetrators; they are exhausted people at the edge of capacity) while still generating a recommendation to access urgent support.
On the next design step: The three practical domains and six psychological domains now form a coherent, integrated architecture. The next logical question is the sequence of development — which features to build first for an MVP that demonstrates value quickly enough to retain users past the first week.
