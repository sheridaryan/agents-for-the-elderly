# Agents for the elderly - Agent for appointment cycle

Domain deep-dive: the appointment cycle — dementia and cognitive decline
Evidence base and clinical rationale
The dementia appointment is one of the most information-dense and emotionally loaded events in informal caregiving, occurring repeatedly across a trajectory that can span a decade or more. The evidence for why it needs dedicated design support is substantial and specific.
Research on dementia caregiving across the disease trajectory shows that even at pre-clinical and mild cognitive stages, family members report worry and anxiety and may initiate care in the form of accompaniment to medical visits and coordination of appointments. National Academies This means the appointment burden begins before formal diagnosis and continues until the elder is in institutional care — it is not an episodic problem but a chronic structural feature of the caregiving role.
The information gap at appointments is well-documented. A systematic review of unmet needs in European dementia care found that both people living with dementia and their caregivers emphasised the necessity for specific information about the disease and available support, including education on medication management and how to address related anxiety. PubMed Central Critically, one qualitative study reported that caregivers felt service providers did not fully comprehend the range of unmet needs, which impacted their ability to fulfil caregiving responsibilities PubMed Central — a finding that names the clinical encounter itself as a site of unmet need.
The unmet needs research is also specific about what caregivers most frequently request. A scoping review of caregiver needs in dementia found that provision of psychoeducational activities tailored to the stage of dementia, and information about coping with behavioural problems and changes, were the most frequently proposed solutions — cited in over half of studies reviewed. ScienceDirect The appointment preparation and debrief cycle is the natural vehicle for delivering both.
From the clinical side, the dynamics within the appointment room present their own well-evidenced problem. Research on family participation in primary care found that cognitive impairment reshapes the caregiver's role from companion to active health manager — a transition that clinicians may not always acknowledge — and that patients held negative views toward approaches where clinicians directed conversation away from them. PubMed Central The caregiver is simultaneously the most informed observer of the elder's day-to-day function and the most structurally undervalued presence in the clinical encounter. The agent's role is to equip them for that asymmetry.
The US policy context is also directly relevant. The CMS GUIDE model, launched July 2024, explicitly mandates care navigation support for dementia caregivers including caregiver training and 24/7 access — using the Zarit Burden Interview as a required screening tool and linking caregiver burden tier to the level of support provided. CMS This means appointment support for dementia caregivers is now part of the US federal reimbursement model — a signal that the clinical community has formally recognised this need, and an opening for an employer benefit product to position itself within that ecosystem.
The validated instrument anchor for this domain is the UNI-C46 (Unmet Needs Instrument for Carers), specifically its "Finding Information" subscale (12 items) and "Managing Dementia Symptoms" subscale (8 items). The UNI-C46 demonstrated acceptable structural and convergent validity and good internal reliability (α = 0.94) across four domains including "Finding information" and "Managing practical needs," with recruitment conducted directly from geriatrician clinics. PubMed Central The agent's pre-appointment preparation maps directly onto these subscales — it is doing conversationally what the UNI-C46 does as a structured assessment.

Phase B1: Pre-appointment preparation
The clinical problem this phase solves. The caregiver holds weeks or months of daily observations in their head — symptoms that appeared and resolved, behaviours that changed in character, new medication side effects, questions that occurred to them during a difficult night. None of this is documented. The clinical encounter begins with a blank information slate on the clinician's side, and the caregiver must reconstruct the relevant history under time pressure, in a context where their own anxiety is elevated. Research consistently shows that caregivers leave appointments feeling they did not say what they needed to say.
Feature 1: Observation harvest. In the 48–72 hours before a scheduled appointment, the agent opens a structured but conversational preparation dialogue. It does not begin with "what symptoms have you noticed?" — a framing that implies the caregiver is a symptom reporter rather than a partner in care. It begins with: "Dr. Azad is on Thursday. Before we pull together your questions — how has your mum been since last time? What's felt most different?" This invites narrative first, then the agent structures what emerges.
The agent draws from its own longitudinal record — incident logs, check-in notes, previous appointment records — and surfaces things the caregiver may have forgotten or not recognised as clinically significant: "You mentioned three weeks ago she was more confused in the mornings than usual — is that still happening? That pattern might be worth raising." This is the practical value of the longitudinal memory: it transforms the agent from a blank-slate chatbot into a clinical partner that remembers.
For dementia specifically, the agent is calibrated to surface observations across four domains that matter most in specialist assessment: cognitive function (memory, orientation, language), behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD — agitation, wandering, sleep disturbance, paranoia), functional independence in activities of daily living, and physical health changes relevant to the condition. These domains mirror the clinical dementia staging tools (CDR, FAST) used by clinicians, so the information the agent helps the caregiver articulate is exactly what the specialist needs.
Feature 2: Question prioritisation. The agent helps the caregiver convert their concerns into a maximum of five questions, ranked by importance. The constraint of five is deliberate — it forces prioritisation, and it is realistic about appointment time. The agent helps with the ranking: "You've mentioned the wandering at night, the new medication timing, and whether she should still be driving. Which of those feels most urgent to you right now?"
Questions are framed in clinician-accessible language without being sanitised of the caregiver's voice. The caregiver's original phrasing — "she keeps accusing me of stealing her things, is this going to get worse?" — becomes: Is the paranoia and accusatory behaviour expected to increase at this stage, and what non-pharmacological strategies are recommended? The agent translates. The caregiver reviews and approves.
Feature 3: The clinical brief. The output of B1 is a structured one-page document — not a form, but a readable narrative with clear sections. It contains: the elder's current status and key changes since the last appointment; the caregiver's observations (date-stamped where relevant); the prioritised question list; and the current medication list with any changes or confusions flagged. The caregiver approves every word before it exists outside the agent. The brief is printable, shareable by the caregiver at their discretion, and filed in the appointment record for debrief reference.
The brief's purpose is twofold. It maximises clinical information transfer in the appointment window. And it positions the caregiver — who often arrives feeling anxious and informationally disorganised — as a prepared, credible, expert observer of the elder's condition. That shift in self-perception is itself clinically meaningful: it reduces the caregiver's pre-appointment anxiety and improves the quality of the clinical encounter.
Psychological integration in B1. Pre-appointment anxiety is near-universal in dementia caregivers, particularly as the disease progresses. The agent acknowledges this explicitly rather than moving straight to task: "These appointments can feel quite loaded — especially when you're not sure what they might say. Is there something specific you're worried about heading in?" This is D1 (validation) at the pre-appointment moment. If the caregiver names a fear — "I'm worried they'll say it's time for memory care" — the agent does not immediately reassure or redirect. It stays with it: "That's a hard thing to hold going into this. Let's make sure you feel as prepared as possible, and we can talk about whatever comes up after."
For distance caregivers specifically, B1 carries additional psychological weight. If they have not attended a recent appointment — which is common — the agent provides contextual catch-up from cohabiting carer notes or from the longitudinal record, so they do not arrive informationally behind. The agent also gently names the guilt that often accompanies the distance caregiver's attendance at a rare appointment: "It means a lot that you're able to be there for this one. You know more about the situation than you might realise — let's make sure that comes through in the room."

Phase B2: In-appointment support
The clinical problem this phase solves. The agent is not in the appointment room. This is a hard boundary — the agent cannot listen, cannot transcribe, cannot interact with the clinician. What it can do is prepare the caregiver for the dynamics of that room, and create a clean, immediate capture pathway the moment they walk out.
Feature 1: Day-of brief and coaching. On the morning of the appointment, the agent sends a concise reminder: the top three questions from the brief, a reminder that the clinical brief is ready to show, and a short note on how to use it: "If it feels useful, you can show Dr. Azad the summary — just say 'I've kept some notes on the changes since last time.' Most clinicians find this helpful."
For dementia appointments specifically, the agent prepares the caregiver for predictable dynamics. The elder may perform better in the clinic than at home (the "clinical visit effect" is well-documented in dementia — familiar environments mask deficits). The agent forewarns: "It's common for people with dementia to seem sharper at clinic appointments than they are at home — that doesn't mean your observations are wrong. Your account of what happens day to day is clinically important even if she appears well today." This psychoeducation directly addresses one of the most demoralising experiences in dementia caregiving: the caregiver watching the elder present coherently while the clinician sees a different person than the one they care for at home.
The agent also coaches on asserting presence. For a caregiver who has historically been talked over or marginalised in appointments, it offers a specific script: "You can introduce yourself at the start and say: 'I'm the primary carer — I want to make sure I understand what's decided today and I have a few questions.' That's enough to set the expectation that you're there as a partner, not just an observer."
Feature 2: Immediate post-appointment capture. Within minutes of the appointment ending — ideally while the caregiver is still in the car park — the agent sends a simple prompt: "How did it go? Tell me while it's fresh — even just a few sentences." This frictionless invitation captures the raw information before processing, family calls, and the elder's own post-appointment distress begin to fragment the account.
The caregiver narrates by voice note or text. The agent structures what it receives into the appointment record: what was said about current status, what was decided or recommended, what follow-up was requested, what the next appointment date is. The caregiver can correct or add before the record is finalised. This is the foundation for the clinical brief for the next appointment — the longitudinal record grows with each cycle.
Psychological integration in B2. The most clinically important psychological acknowledgement in this phase addresses what happens when the clinician talks to the elder as though the caregiver is not present, or dismisses the caregiver's account because the elder presented well. Research identifies this directly: patients held negative views when clinicians directed conversation away from them, and clinicians reported using a family-focused approach in dementia contexts as a means of expediting the visit — a dynamic that is uncomfortable for both parties. PubMed Central When the debrief reveals this pattern, the agent names it: "It sounds like you felt a bit invisible in there — that happens more than it should. Your observations about what happens at home are clinically valid even when she seems fine in clinic." This is D1 validation applied specifically to the clinical context, where caregivers are most structurally disempowered.

Phase B3: Post-appointment debrief
The clinical problem this phase solves. After the appointment, the caregiver is holding several things simultaneously: clinical information received (some of it distressing), emotional processing of news about progression, follow-up logistics to manage (a referral, a prescription change, a blood test), and the task of relaying all of this to other family members who were not present. They do this alone, often while also managing the elder's post-appointment agitation or fatigue. No current tool addresses this moment.
Feature 1: Clinical follow-up capture. The structured debrief begins with the practical: "Let's capture what was decided before anything slips. What did the doctor say about the new medication? Was the memory clinic referral confirmed? When's the next appointment?" This practical grounding serves a psychological function — it gives the caregiver something concrete and manageable to do with their distress, and completing it creates a clean transition to the emotional debrief.
The output is a timestamped decision record and a follow-up checklist: "Referral to memory clinic — GP to send letter by [date]. Blood test to book by next Friday. New acetylcholinesterase inhibitor prescription — confirm timing with pharmacist." These reappear as reminders at appropriate intervals.
Feature 2: Family update draft. The agent generates a draft update for the care network — siblings, other family members, close friends who are part of the caregiving circle — drawn from the appointment record. The caregiver reviews and edits it. The draft is human and readable, not clinical: "We saw the neurologist today. He confirmed Mum's dementia has progressed to the moderate stage — she's doing well with daily support but is likely to need more help over the next year. He's referred her to the memory clinic for a full review. We're managing it, and I'll share more as we know it."
This feature directly addresses one of the most exhausting hidden labours in caregiving: the repeated task of individually re-explaining the situation to different family members, each at a different level of understanding, each with their own emotional response that the caregiver must then manage. The draft does not eliminate this entirely, but it reduces the energy required.
Feature 3: Emotional debrief. Only once the practical debrief is complete — or immediately if the caregiver signals distress before it is — does the agent shift register: "Before we finish — how are you doing after all of that?" This question is the threshold between the practical and psychological layers. It is always offered, never forced. Some caregivers will say "fine, just tired" and that is a complete and valid answer. Others will need the space.
The psychological domains activated by the debrief depend on what happened in the appointment. If the clinician confirmed progression, D2 (grief and ambiguous loss) is likely activated — the caregiver is grieving the elder as they once were, again, with new specificity. The agent can hold this: "Hearing that it's progressed — even when you've been expecting it — can hit differently than you thought it would. There's no right way to take that in." If the caregiver expresses guilt about their advocacy — "I forgot to ask about the driving, I should have pushed harder on the medication" — D6 (cognitive reappraisal) is the appropriate response: "You asked three of your five questions in a ten-minute appointment. That's not a failure — that's a real constraint. The driving question is now at the top of your list for next time."
The debrief is also the primary burnout signal detection moment in this domain. Language like "I don't know how much more of this I can do," "I just feel completely alone with it all," or acute distress following bad news are D5 signals. The agent's passive detection layer registers these. The five-level escalation protocol remains on standby throughout.

Feature specification for the developer
This specification describes what needs to be built, not how to build it. Implementation decisions (API structure, database schema, UI framework) are for the developer.
Data model — appointment record. Each appointment generates a structured record containing: appointment date and type (GP, neurologist, memory clinic, geriatrician); elder's current status summary; observations since last appointment (structured by domain: cognitive, behavioural/BPSD, ADL, physical); question list (max 5, ranked); clinical brief (text, approved flag, share history); appointment outcome (decisions made, follow-up items with due dates, next appointment); debrief notes (practical capture, emotional layer if any, burden signal flag if triggered). The appointment record links to the longitudinal caregiving situation model and to the incident log, which may have generated observations that feed into the clinical brief.
Conversation flow — B1 trigger. Trigger: appointment date exists in the agent's record AND current time is 72–48 hours before. Agent initiates the preparation conversation. The conversation has five stages: (1) warm open — how has the elder been; (2) observation harvest — drawing from longitudinal record, prompting by domain; (3) question generation — converting observations to questions, caregiver prioritises; (4) brief generation — agent drafts, caregiver reviews and approves; (5) anxiety acknowledgement — is there anything the caregiver is worried they might hear? All stages are conversational, not form-like. The caregiver can exit any stage and return.
Conversation flow — B2 trigger. Trigger: appointment date is today. Agent sends morning brief (top 3 questions, encouragement, coaching note appropriate to appointment type and caregiver type — cohabiting or distance). Post-appointment trigger: 30–60 minutes after appointment end time (estimated), agent sends: "How did it go? Tell me while it's fresh." Voice capture or text accepted. Agent structures the narrative into the appointment record.
Conversation flow — B3 trigger. Trigger: post-appointment capture received. Agent initiates debrief conversation. Sequence is fixed: practical first, emotional second. Practical covers: decisions made, follow-up items, next appointment. Family update draft is generated and offered. Emotional debrief is offered as a distinct transition — agent explicitly names the shift: "We've got the practical side captured. Before we close — how are you doing after all of that?" Burden signal detection (D5 passive layer) runs throughout the debrief conversation.
Bifurcation logic. At intake, the agent establishes whether the caregiver is cohabiting or at distance. Cohabiting B1 includes: logistics (how are you getting there, who is staying with the elder if needed); elder anxiety management (how does she usually respond to clinic visits). Distance B1 includes: context-building from cohabiting carer notes or longitudinal record; guilt reframe for not attending more often. This bifurcation flag is stored in the situation model and applies across all three phases.
Clinical boundary enforcement. The clinical brief is a document the caregiver produces, owns, and shares at their discretion. The agent does not transmit any document to any clinician. The agent does not send appointment reminders to clinicians. The agent does not create, modify, or interact with any electronic health record system. These are hard constraints, not soft guidelines. The agent is explicitly positioned as a caregiver support tool, not a clinical communication platform. This boundary must be disclosed to the user at onboarding and reinforced at each appointment cycle.
Escalation protocol integration. The D5 passive detection layer runs in the background during all three phases of the appointment cycle. Specific triggers: acute distress language in the immediate post-appointment window; expressions of hopelessness or inability to continue in the debrief; disclosure of self-harm ideation at any point. These follow the five-level graduated escalation framework established in Domain 5. The appointment cycle does not have its own escalation pathway — it feeds into the universal D5 protocol.
Storage requirements. Appointment records are stored in the longitudinal situation model with full timestamp history. The caregiver can view their own appointment history at any time. The employer reporting layer (Phase 6) has access only to anonymised, aggregate appointment frequency data — never to appointment content, questions asked, or clinical information discussed. This technical architecture must be established before any employer-facing reporting is built.

Sample agent conversations
Three illustrative exchanges — one from each phase — showing the integration of practical function and psychological support in a realistic voice.
________________________________________
B1 — Pre-appointment preparation, cohabiting daughter caregiver, moderate dementia:
Agent: "Dad's appointment with Dr. Singh at the memory clinic is on Thursday morning. Before we put together your questions, how has he been these past few weeks? What's felt most different?"
Caregiver: "Honestly it's been hard. He's been getting up at night, wandering, and last Tuesday he didn't recognise me for about ten minutes. That was new. Really scared me."
Agent: "That must have been frightening — not recognising you is one of those moments that can stop you cold. Thank you for telling me that. It's also an important observation for Thursday. The night-time wandering and the episode of not recognising you — were they connected, or did the recognition issue happen separately?"
Caregiver: "Separately. He was fine after breakfast. Just that one morning."
Agent: "That detail matters — that it resolved and happened in the morning rather than at night. I'll make sure it's noted carefully. Is there anything else that's changed since his last appointment in January?"
Caregiver: "He's been more agitated in the evenings. And the new medication — I'm not sure if it's helping or making things worse."
Agent: "Three important things: the nocturnal wandering, the transient recognition episode, and uncertainty about the medication response. Let me add those to the brief and help you turn them into questions. Which of these feels most urgent to raise on Thursday?"

B2 — Day-of, distance caregiver daughter flying in for a rare neurology appointment:
Agent: "Today's the day — neurology with Dr. Okonkwo at 10. Your top three questions are ready: the medication adjustment, whether the disease stage has changed since last year, and next steps for care planning. A few things to keep in mind: it's very common for people with dementia to seem sharper in clinic than at home — that doesn't undermine anything you've observed. If Dr. Okonkwo seems to be assessing your mum rather than asking you anything, it's completely reasonable to say: 'I'm the main carer — can I share some observations from the past few months?' You've got the brief. Trust what you know."
Later, 45 minutes after the appointment end time:
Agent: "How did it go? Tell me while it's fresh — even just the headline. What did he say?"

B3 — Post-appointment debrief, grief activated, confirmation of progression to moderate stage:
Agent: "Let's get the practical side captured first. He confirmed the progression to moderate stage — is there anything else he said that felt significant? Any changes to the medication? Was the occupational therapy referral confirmed?"
Caregiver: "Yes — referral to OT, and a letter to the GP about the driving. He thinks she needs to stop driving."
Agent: "Important things. I've noted: OT referral — expect a call within 4–6 weeks; driving — GP letter to be sent, conversation to have with your mum. Do you want me to draft a brief update for your brother and sister so you don't have to re-explain this three times?"
Caregiver: "Yes please. I can't face those calls right now."
Agent: "Absolutely. I'll draft something and you can look at it when you're ready. Before we close this — how are you doing after all of that? Hearing it's moved to moderate stage, even when you've been watching it happen — that's a lot to take in while driving home."
Caregiver: "I just keep thinking about the version of her from five years ago. She wouldn't even recognise herself now."
Agent: "That's a real grief. The person she was is still there in some ways — and also genuinely changed. Both of those things are true at the same time, and it's one of the hardest parts of this particular kind of loss. You don't have to resolve it today."
